Vultr vs DigitalOcean

Choose Vultr when its price, location or network configuration fixes a specific limitation in your DigitalOcean setup. Choose DigitalOcean when a trial resolves the problem you are having on Vultr. If your existing system works, establish what the move will improve before migrating it.

The decision changes with the workload. A cheaper machine can justify moving a small application. A cluster with unreliable internal connections needs a different comparison. Credits can even send the same person back to the provider they previously left.

The prices and configurations below describe individual historical setups, with the year attached. They are not current quotes. The practical recommendations are judgments drawn from those cases, rather than promises about either provider.

Current performance: no same-workload result verified

We have not verified a current, like-for-like performance benchmark for Vultr and DigitalOcean. The migration accounts below describe dated individual experiences from 2015 to 2024. They do not establish either provider's current speed, reliability, or overall performance, and we have not run an equivalent test.

For a useful comparison, run the same application and workload on specific plans in the regions you would actually use. Record the plan, region, test date, client location, repeated latency results, throughput, and disk I/O. Keep the software and test settings the same, and report the measurements rather than turning one result into a provider-wide ranking.

When the lower price makes Vultr worth considering

In a migration described in 2020, DigitalOcean had offered 512MB RAM for $5, while Vultr had offered the same memory for $2.50. Price helped trigger the move to Vultr.

There was another reason: files in DigitalOcean object storage had reportedly become unavailable for several hours every few weeks. This was an object storage problem. It does not establish that the virtual machines themselves were going down.

By 2020, the Vultr account had been in use for three years. The experience was described as trouble free apart from a billing error that applied Australian VAT to a customer living in Serbia. Usage subsequently expanded to more instances and services.

That combination matters: the move addressed both a small server bill and a separate service problem. It gives you two things to examine in your own setup, the recurring charge and the component that is actually failing. It does not establish a reliability ranking for all DigitalOcean or Vultr products.

A different 2017 move involved a $10 DigitalOcean machine in New York and a $5 Vultr machine in New York. The listed CPUs were 1.8GHz and 2.4GHz respectively, while the disks were 30GB and 25GB.

The trade was concrete. The Vultr machine cost less and had a smaller disk. CPU clock speed alone cannot establish how much faster an application will run, so those specifications do not establish a performance multiplier.

For a small application, compare the resources it needs before choosing the cheaper configuration. In that 2017 example, moving to the lower price also meant accepting less disk space.

When DigitalOcean solves the more expensive problem

A migration documented in 2024 went in the opposite direction, after about five years on Vultr.

The affected setup used AMD High Performance shared compute in New Jersey, including machines with 4GB, 8GB and 16GB RAM. Its operator described packet loss and intermittent networking problems across employer, side business and personal accounts. Their approximate monthly spending was $500, $1,100 and $50 respectively. Those were account totals, not individual VM prices.

One symptom was particularly misleading. Zabbix marked PHP-FPM as down because it could not reliably reach the status page over the private network. The process itself was still running.

Around 30 support tickets and repeated moves between host machines preceded the switch. DigitalOcean was tested for one or two months before the completed migration, and the operator described the trial as stable. The later update also described better performance and disk I/O, without enough underlying measurements to reproduce a percentage improvement.

For a similar cluster, my recommendation is to test the internal connections your application actually depends on. Include monitoring traffic. A successful public website check would not, by itself, reproduce the private network problem in this case.

The 2020 move to Vultr and the 2024 move to DigitalOcean point in opposite directions. They also involve different services, locations and workloads. Use that distinction when deciding what to test.

When RAM misses the reason for moving

In a migration described in 2018, Vultr had offered 768MB RAM for $5 while DigitalOcean had offered 512MB. Yet the stated reason for switching was the IPv6 allocation: a /64 at Vultr versus a /128 at DigitalOcean at the time.

The extra memory was part of the comparison, but the network configuration drove the decision. Those historical allocations do not establish what either provider supplies today. If IPv6 is essential to your deployment, confirm the current allocation and the behaviour your application requires before moving it.

Location changed another decision in 2017. A developer in Tampa moved from a DigitalOcean setup to Vultr, valued its Miami location and described the new configuration as 2GB for $10.

That move also involved replacing a separate managed database service with a self managed MongoDB instance. The connection drops and replica problems described before the move belonged to that separate service. They cannot be charged to DigitalOcean's compute platform.

This was a change of both provider and database arrangement. My reading is that it cannot isolate which change improved the application. If you make both changes together, keep that uncertainty in your own assessment too.

Credits can reverse an otherwise sensible move

In 2015, one sequence ran from DigitalOcean to Vultr and then back to DigitalOcean. The initial move involved FreeBSD availability and dissatisfaction with DigitalOcean's San Francisco network. The return happened after obtaining DigitalOcean credits.

The same account compared a $5 tier with 512MB at DigitalOcean and 768MB at Vultr. Vultr's memory advantage had not disappeared from the reasoning. Credits had changed the immediate cost of using DigitalOcean.

For that situation, I would make two budget comparisons: what you pay while credit remains, and what you pay after it runs out. A temporary balance can reasonably affect where you run a project. It does not settle the ongoing cost comparison.

digitalocean vs vultr for an existing deployment

Choose a trial around the reason you are considering a move. For the 2024 cluster, that reason was unreliable internal networking. For the migration described in 2018, it was an IPv6 requirement. For the 2017 New York comparison, lower cost came with a smaller disk.

My recommendation is to write down the result that would justify moving: reliable status checks across your private network, the required network configuration, or a lower bill for resources your application can use. Then test that condition before cutting over.

vultr vs digital ocean for a new project

Start with the constraints you can name: memory, disk, location and network requirements. Compare the current configurations that meet them, and include the cost after any credit expires.

Keep separate decisions visible. A database redesign, a different location and a new server provider can all affect the outcome. The useful result is knowing which change fixed your problem, so you can recognise whether a later migration would help.
